Grammar

01

Parts of speech and syntactic categories

Word classes describe how words behave in structure. Form, meaning, and distribution all provide evidence; a word's role in one sentence does not always determine its class in every use.

Open lexical classes

Nouns head noun phrases; verbs head verb phrases; adjectives modify nouns or function as complements; adverbs modify or qualify phrases and clauses. These classes readily admit new members.

Closed and functional classes

Determiners specify noun phrases; pronouns can function as noun phrases; prepositions head preposition phrases; auxiliaries support tense, aspect, voice, and modality. Conjunctions connect units.

Category depends on use

Compare They record the result (verb) with Keep a record (noun). Distribution is a useful diagnostic: ask what positions a word can occupy and what elements it combines with.

02

Tenses and aspects

Study each form separately by time reference and aspect. These 12 teaching patterns combine present, past, and future reference with simple, progressive, perfect, and perfect progressive forms.

English marks present and past tense inflectionally; future time is expressed through constructions such as will and be going to. The 12-form chart is a learner framework, not a claim that English has 12 inflectional tenses.

Present-time forms

Present simple

base verb; he/she/it + -s

Use: habits, facts, and regular states.

Maya teaches linguistics.

Present progressive

am/is/are + verb-ing

Use: actions in progress or temporary situations.

Maya is teaching a seminar this week.

Present perfect

have/has + past participle

Use: past events linked to the present; experience or result.

Maya has taught three seminars this term.

Present perfect progressive

have/has been + verb-ing

Use: activity continuing to now or recently completed with duration in focus.

Maya has been teaching since noon.

Past-time forms

Past simple

past form of the verb

Use: completed situations located in past time.

Maya taught the class yesterday.

Past progressive

was/were + verb-ing

Use: an activity in progress at a past reference time.

Maya was teaching when the alarm rang.

Past perfect

had + past participle

Use: a situation earlier than another past point.

Maya had left before the lecture began.

Past perfect progressive

had been + verb-ing

Use: duration or ongoing activity leading up to a past point.

Maya had been teaching for an hour when I arrived.

Future-time forms

Future simple

will + base; am/is/are going to + base

Use: predictions, decisions, intentions, or plans; context helps distinguish them.

I will email you. / We are going to revise the draft.

Future progressive

will be + verb-ing

Use: an activity in progress at a future reference time.

At ten tomorrow, Maya will be teaching.

Future perfect

will have + past participle

Use: a situation completed before a future deadline or point.

By Friday, Maya will have finished the unit.

Future perfect progressive

will have been + verb-ing

Use: duration continuing up to a future point.

By June, Maya will have been teaching here for a year.

Auxiliary order: modal + perfect have + progressive be + main verb: may have been waiting. Each auxiliary contributes a distinct grammatical meaning.
03

Phrases and clauses

A phrase is organized around a head. A clause typically contains a predicate and its dependents; clauses may be finite or non-finite, independent or subordinate.

Noun phrase (NP)

Built around a noun or pronoun. In those two detailed reports, reports is the head, with a determiner and modifiers.

Verb phrase (VP)

Built around a verb, with auxiliaries and complements as required: might have completed the survey.

Preposition phrase (PP)

Built around a preposition and commonly followed by a complement: under the bridge, after the meeting.

Adjective and adverb phrases

An adjective or adverb heads its phrase and may be modified or complemented: very proud of the result; quite slowly.

CLAUSEcontainsSUBJECT: NP+PREDICATE: VP

Finite clauses

A finite verb phrase is marked for tense or modality and can anchor a clause: The committee approved the plan. A main clause can stand independently, while a subordinate clause depends on a larger structure.

Non-finite clauses

Non-finite clauses use infinitive, gerund-participial, or past-participial forms: To finish on time matters; After reading the report, Maya replied.

04

Sentence structures

Clause patterns describe how a verb combines with subjects, objects, and complements. These are structural patterns, not universal templates for every language.

Statements

Declarative clauses commonly present information. Their form does not guarantee that the utterance functions only as an assertion.

Questions

English yes/no questions commonly use subject-auxiliary inversion: Has the team arrived? Wh-questions place a questioned phrase at the front: Where did they meet?

Commands

Imperative clauses commonly have an unexpressed subject understood as you: Please review the final section.

05

Core grammar rules

A practical rule collection for common English patterns. Each rule has its own box with a form or diagnostic and a new example.

Basic clause order

Subject + Verb + (Object / Complement)

English statements commonly place the subject before the verb. Objects and complements follow according to the verb pattern.

The tutor explained the rule.

Subject-verb agreement

he/she/it + present lexical verb -s

In the present simple, a third-person singular subject usually takes an -s form. Match the verb to the grammatical subject, not a nearby noun.

The list of examples is on the page.

Articles and countability

a/an + singular count noun; the + identifiable noun

Use a/an when introducing one non-specific singular count noun. Use the when the referent is identifiable; many non-count nouns do not take a/an.

She found a source and cited the source.

Negation and do-support

do/does/did + not + base verb

When a simple present or past clause has no other auxiliary, English normally uses do-support for negation. Keep the main verb in its base form after do.

He does not submit late work.

Questions and inversion

Auxiliary + Subject + Main verb?

Yes/no questions commonly place an auxiliary before the subject. If there is no auxiliary in a simple present or past, use do, does, or did.

Did the students understand?

Modal verbs

modal + base verb

Core modal auxiliaries do not take -s and are followed by a base-form verb. They express meanings such as possibility, obligation, ability, or prediction.

The result may indicate a pattern.

Adjective position

Determiner + Adjective + Noun

Attributive adjectives commonly precede a noun in English. Some adjectives also occur after linking verbs as subject complements.

A careful analysis; the analysis is careful.

Pronoun reference

Pronoun reference should be recoverable

A pronoun should have a clear antecedent and normally agree with it in person and number. Revise when two possible antecedents make the reference unclear.

Lina gave Noor her notes. (Whose notes?)

Prepositions and complements

Preposition + Noun Phrase / Clause

A preposition heads a phrase and can take a complement. The choice of preposition is often conventional and may depend on the head word.

They relied on the evidence.

These are useful generalizations, not exception-free formulas. Check verb pattern, meaning, register, and the variety of English being described.

06

Structural transformations

Related clause patterns can reorganize information while preserving important parts of an event or proposition. The transformations below are common English constructions.

Active to passive

The passive places the active object in subject position and uses be + past participle. The agent phrase may be omitted when unknown, obvious, or less important.

The editor revised the article.→The article was revised by the editor.

Statement to yes/no question

Move an existing auxiliary before the subject. If a simple present or past statement has no auxiliary, add do-support.

The class has finished.→Has the class finished?

Wh-question formation

Place the questioned phrase at the front. In many questions, an auxiliary precedes the subject; subject questions have a different pattern.

They met after class.→When did they meet?

Relative-clause embedding

A relative clause modifies a noun phrase and can identify or add information about its referent.

I read the article. It won the award.→I read the article that won the award.

Analyses of movement and clause structure differ across grammatical frameworks. These examples illustrate forms and functions without assuming one theoretical account.
