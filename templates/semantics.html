LANGUAGE LAB / 05

Semantics: meaning in language

Semantics studies how words, phrases, and sentences carry meaning, how those meanings connect, and how context shapes interpretation in real communication.

Sense & Reference Semiotic Triangle Relations Roles Utterance Sandbox References
01

Sense and reference

Meaning is not only a dictionary idea; it is also shaped by use, context, and speaker intention.

Sense

Sense is the mental or conceptual meaning of an expression. It explains how a word fits into a system of relations with other words and how speakers understand its content.

  • Conceptual structure
  • Associations and contrasts
  • Word-internal meaning relations

Reference

Reference is the connection between a linguistic form and the actual entity, event, or concept it points to in the real world. A proper noun may refer to a single person, place, or institution.

  • Direct pointing
  • Actual world connection
  • Context-dependent identification

Example: The expression the morning star and the evening star may refer to the same object in the sky, but the sense is differently highlighted through context and cultural association.

02

The semiotic triangle

The sign does not connect directly to the world without an interpretive layer of thought and convention.

Symbol

The word form or linguistic sign: dog, tree, water.

Thought / reference

The mental representation created by the speaker or listener; it is the conceptual understanding activated by the symbol.

Referent

The actual object, action, or entity in the real world that the symbol points to.

03

Semantic relations

Words are organized through networks of similarity, contrast, inclusion, and ambiguity.

Synonymy

Words with closely related meanings in a given context; they may be near-equivalents rather than perfect replacements.

big large quick fast

Antonymy

Opposites can be complementary, gradable, or directional.

dead alive hot cold up down
  • Complementary: on/off, single/married
  • Gradable: rich/poor, hot/cold
  • Directional: come/go, buy/sell

Hyponymy

A more specific item is a hyponym of a broader category.

Animal
Mammal

Polysemy

One word has several related senses within a common conceptual field.

head of a person of a company of a river

Homonymy

Different words share the same form but are unrelated semantically.

bat animal sports implement
04

Semantic roles

Participants in events are interpreted according to the role they play in an action or state.

Agent

The initiator of the action.

The student opened the book.

Theme

The entity that is affected, moved, or experienced.

The student opened the book.

Instrument

The means used to carry out the action.

She cut the paper with a knife.

Experiencer

The entity that feels, perceives, or experiences a state.

Mariam felt nervous during the lecture.

Location

The place in which the event happens.

They met at the library.

Source

The starting point of motion or transfer.

The letter came from London.

Goal

The endpoint of motion or transfer.

The package moved to the office.

06

Utterance: meaning in a real situation

An utterance is a concrete use of language by a speaker or writer at a particular time and place, addressed to an audience for a purpose.

Sentence vs. utterance

A sentence is an abstract grammatical construction. An utterance is a particular occurrence of language in a communicative event. The same sentence can be uttered repeatedly by different people, at different times, with different referents or intentions.

Same sentence, different utterances

I am ready now. The speaker, the time of now, and what they are ready for depend on each occasion of use.

Utterance vs. proposition

A proposition is the content that can be evaluated as true or false. An utterance is the situated act through which a speaker expresses that content. Different utterances can express the same proposition, while one sentence form can express different propositions in different contexts.

Context fixes the proposition

She is here. To identify the proposition, a listener needs to know who she is and which place counts as here.

How context contributes to meaning

  • Deixis: expressions such as I, you, here, and tomorrow are interpreted relative to the speech event.
  • Reference: listeners identify which person or thing a phrase picks out using shared knowledge and the situation.
  • Ellipsis: omitted material can be recovered from context, as in Want some?
  • Ambiguity: context can help select among multiple readings, though it does not always remove every ambiguity.
  • Prosody and gesture: stress, intonation, facial expression, and pointing can guide interpretation.

Utterance meaning and speaker meaning

Sentence meaning comes from conventional linguistic forms and composition. Utterance meaning combines that conventional content with the context of use. Speaker meaning is what the speaker intends the audience to understand or recognize.

A contextual inference

At an open window, It is cold in here can describe the temperature and, in an appropriate context, indirectly suggest closing the window.

Utterances as speech acts

In saying something, speakers can also perform an action. A useful introductory distinction separates the words said, the act performed in saying them, and the possible effect on a listener.

Locutionary act

The production of a meaningful linguistic expression with a conventional sense and reference.

The words: Can you pass the salt?

Illocutionary act

The communicative force or act performed, such as requesting, promising, warning, or asserting.

The intended act: a polite request.

Perlocutionary effect

The effect the utterance actually has on a listener, which may or may not match the speaker's intention.

Possible effect: the listener passes the salt.

Direct and indirect acts: Pass the salt, please is a direct request. Could you pass the salt? has interrogative form but can function as an indirect request. Whether it succeeds depends on context, shared conventions, and how the addressee understands it.
