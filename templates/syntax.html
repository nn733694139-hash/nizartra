Syntax: how sentences are built

Explore how words combine into phrases and clauses, how grammatical rules describe those structures, and how one sentence can support more than one reading.

THE CENTRAL IDEA

A sentence is more than a string of words.

Syntax studies the hierarchical relationships between words. In a simple clause, a noun phrase and a verb phrase combine to form a sentence.

S→NP+VP
01

Fundamentals of syntax

Three influential ideas for describing sentence structure.

Generative grammar

A grammar is a finite system of principles that can characterize an open-ended range of sentences. Generative approaches ask what speakers know about the structure of their language, including dependencies that are not visible in word order alone.

FINITE RULES→MANY SENTENCES
STRUCTURE→INTERPRETATION

Competence is a speaker's implicit knowledge; actual speech is performance, which can include pauses, repairs, and memory effects.

Phrase structure rules

Rewrite rules describe which constituents can expand a phrase. The arrow means “can be expanded as”; parentheses mark optional material and braces mark alternatives.

S→NP VPclause
NP→(Det) (AdjP) Nnoun phrase
VP→V (NP) (PP)verb phrase

For example, The researcher examined the sample expands as S → NP VP, then each phrase expands into smaller constituents.

X-bar theory

A general template relates a phrase's head to its complement and specifier. X stands for a category such as N, V, or P; X′ is an intermediate projection, and XP is the maximal phrase.

XP
SpecifierX′
Head XComplement

The head determines the phrase's category: N heads NP, V heads VP, and P heads PP. A complement is selected by, and structurally close to, its head.

Head and complement

The head contributes the core category and selection requirements. A complement completes the head's meaning and is typically its sister in a phrase marker.

HEAD: VputCOMPLEMENT: NPthe book
HEAD: PonCOMPLEMENT: NPthe shelf

Put requires a theme and a location phrase; on heads the PP and takes the shelf as its complement.

02

Break a sentence into constituents

A step-by-step derivation makes the hierarchy visible.

WORKED EXAMPLE

The new student will read the article in the library.

Start with the clause rule, identify the subject and predicate, then expand each phrase until its words are reached.

  1. S → NP VP: divide the clause into subject and predicate.
  2. NP → Det AdjP N: The new student is the subject.
  3. VP → Aux VP: will is the auxiliary; read... is the lexical VP.
  4. VP → V NP PP: the verb, its object, and the location phrase form the predicate.
Bracketed constituent structure [S [NP [Det The] [AdjP [Adj new]] [N student]] [VP [Aux will] [VP [V read] [NP [Det the] [N article]] [PP [P in] [NP [Det the] [N library]]]]]]

Brackets show grouping: the PP is attached to the VP here and gives the location of the reading event.

04

Ambiguity and transformations

Structure can change interpretation, and transformations can relate sentence forms.

Structural ambiguity

A sentence is structurally ambiguous when its words allow more than one constituent grouping.

SentenceI saw the man with a telescope.
VP attachmentI used a telescope to see the man.
NP attachmentThe man I saw had a telescope.

Transformational grammar

Movement rules describe dependencies between a phrase's pronounced position and its interpreted position. In many current analyses, a moved phrase is linked to a lower copy or gap; movement is constrained, not free word shuffling.

ACTIVEThe student read the book.
→
PASSIVEThe book was read by the student.

The object moves into subject position, and the verb takes passive form.

BASEThe student read the book.
MOVEthe book → subject position
REALIZEbe + past participle
