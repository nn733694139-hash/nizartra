Morphology: how words are built

Explore the smallest meaningful units in language, how they combine, and how words change form or meaning.

THE IDEA

Morphology studies how words are built.

A word can contain a root and smaller meaningful parts called morphemes. Affixes attach to a base to change meaning or grammar.

un- + happy + -ness = unhappiness

    Morpheme types

    A morpheme is the smallest unit that carries meaning or grammatical function.

    Free morphemes

    Can stand alone as a word. They include lexical roots and grammatical words.

    bookgreenandquickly

    Bound morphemes

    Must attach to another form. Roots such as -ceive and affixes such as un- are bound.

    Allomorphy: one morpheme, different forms

    The plural and past-tense morphemes change pronunciation according to their sound environment.

    These are conditioned allomorphs: the choice follows a phonological rule. Irregular forms may follow other patterns.

    06

    Morphological features and rules

    Roots, bases and stems combine with grammatical features under language-specific constraints.

    Root · base · stem

    Root: the irreducible lexical core. Base: any form an affix attaches to. Stem: the form to which inflection attaches. These terms describe different roles, not always different strings.

    Inflectional features

    English marks number (cat/cats), tense (walk/walked), aspect (walk/walking), person (I walk/she walks), degree (small/smaller), and case (I/me). Other languages may mark gender, mood, evidentiality, or richer agreement.

    Affixation and order

    Affixes attach to bases in constrained orders. In un-happi-ness, derivational material builds a word; inflection can attach outside it, as in unhappiness-es.

    Productivity and blocking

    A productive rule can form new words (re-post). Productivity is not unlimited: phonology, meaning, word class and existing forms constrain which combinations speakers accept.

    Compounding structure

    Compounds combine lexical bases. English compounds may be written closed (toothbrush), open (post office) or hyphenated (well-known); spelling alone does not determine structure.

    Morphological trees

    Trees represent hierarchical grouping, not just a sequence of letters. Bracketing can distinguish [unlock]able (“can be unlocked”) from un[lockable] (“not lockable”).
