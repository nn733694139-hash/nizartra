Discourse Analysis

Discourse analysis studies language beyond the sentence: how speakers and writers create connected texts, accomplish social actions, manage interaction, and shape meaning in context.

Cohesion & Coherence Speech Acts & Grice Conversation Analysis Context Analysis Critical Discourse Analysis References
01

Cohesion and coherence

Cohesion describes the linguistic ties that connect clauses and sentences. Coherence is the meaningful relationship readers or listeners construct across a text.

Reference and substitution

Reference points to an entity in the text or situation: Maya arrived. She sat down. Substitution replaces a repeated item: I need a pen. Do you have one?

Ellipsis

Ellipsis leaves recoverable material unspoken: Want some tea? The omitted subject and auxiliary are understood from the exchange.

Conjunction and lexical cohesion

Conjunction signals relations such as addition, contrast, cause, or sequence. Lexical cohesion links words through repetition, synonymy, hyponymy, or related vocabulary.

Example: The team reviewed the proposal. However, they postponed it because the cost was unclear. The pronouns create reference ties; however signals contrast; because introduces a reason. Coherence depends on how the reader connects the review, cost, and decision.

Discourse markers

Discourse markers help signal how an utterance relates to the surrounding talk or text. They guide interpretation and interaction, but do not by themselves guarantee that a text is coherent.

Addition: moreoverContrast: howeverConsequence: thereforeStance: franklySequence: firstResponse: well
02

Speech Act Theory and Grice's Cooperative Principle

Utterances do things as well as say things. Their force depends on conventions, intentions, and the circumstances in which they are used.

Locutionary act

The act of producing a meaningful linguistic expression with a conventional sense and reference.

Illocutionary act

The communicative force performed in saying something: asserting, requesting, promising, apologizing, or naming.

Perlocutionary effect

The effect an utterance has on a hearer, such as persuading, reassuring, surprising, or prompting action.

Words"The floor is wet."
Possible forceA warning, depending on speaker intention and the situation.
Possible effectThe listener avoids the area. Effects are possible, not guaranteed.

Grice's four conversational maxims

Quantity

Give as much information as the exchange requires: neither too little nor needlessly more.

A route question normally calls for the directions needed, not an unrelated history of the street.

Quality

Try to contribute what you believe to be true and have adequate grounds to support.

Do not present a guess as a verified fact.

Relation

Make contributions relevant to the purposes and current direction of the conversation.

A reply about the weather may be relevant if it explains why a meeting moved indoors.

Manner

Be clear and orderly; avoid unnecessary obscurity and ambiguity.

Give the steps in the order someone needs to follow them.
How implicature works

When a contribution appears to depart from a maxim, listeners often infer an additional meaning while assuming the exchange remains interpretable. In Some of the reviewers agreed, a hearer may infer that not all agreed, depending on context. This is a cancellable conversational implicature, not a literal entailment.

Maxims are analytic expectations, not rules that every speaker always follows. A maxim may be observed, quietly violated, openly flouted, or overridden by a stronger consideration.

03

Conversation Analysis

Conversation Analysis (CA) studies the organization of naturally occurring interaction, including turn-taking, sequence, and repair.

Adjacency pairs

An adjacency pair is a coordinated sequence of two turns: a first pair-part makes a relevant second pair-part conditionally expected. Common types include greeting-greeting, question-answer, offer-acceptance, and request-granting or refusal.

Pairs can include an insertion sequence, and their parts need not be immediately adjacent when clarification or other interaction intervenes.

Preferred and dispreferred responses

In CA, preference describes a structural tendency, not a speaker's personal liking. Acceptances are often brief and direct; refusals may be delayed, softened, or accompanied by accounts.

These are patterns to investigate in recordings, not universal formulas for every language or community.

QuestionA: "Could you send me the notes?" AnswerB: "Sure, I'll send them after class." StructureThe request makes granting or refusal relevant; the response grants and adds a time commitment.

Turn-taking

Participants project possible completion points and negotiate who speaks next. Overlap and silence are analyzed in their sequential setting.

Repair

Repair practices address problems in speaking, hearing, or understanding. Speakers may self-correct, or another participant may initiate clarification.

Sequence organization

Openings, closings, pre-sequences, and insert expansions shape what actions become relevant next.

04

Context analysis

Interpretation depends on more than sentence meaning. Analyze who is speaking, to whom, where, for what purpose, through which medium, and against what shared knowledge.

ParticipantsSpeaker, audience, roles, relationships, and relative authority.
SettingTime, place, activity, and the material circumstances of the exchange.
PurposeWhat participants are trying to accomplish and what outcomes are relevant.
Channel and genreSpoken or written mode, platform, audience expectations, and genre conventions.
Context-sensitive example: "It's cold in here" can be a report about temperature, a complaint, or an indirect request to close a window. The wording alone does not determine which action is intended.
Deixis: What do words such as here, now, and you refer to in this situation?
Common ground: What knowledge do participants treat as shared?
Register: How do field, tenor, and mode shape vocabulary and style?
Inference: Which interpretations are supported, and what evidence would distinguish them?
05

Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA)

CDA examines how discourse participates in representing social life, sustaining or challenging power relations, and making some perspectives more visible than others.

TextVocabulary, grammar, agency, evaluation, metaphor, and the organization of a text.
Discursive practiceHow texts are produced, circulated, interpreted, quoted, and connected to other texts.
Social practiceThe institutions, histories, ideologies, and power relations in which discourse is situated.

Representation

Who is named, categorized, foregrounded, or left unspecified?

Agency

Who is represented as acting, and who appears only as affected by events?

Evaluation

Which words express certainty, obligation, approval, blame, or distance?

Use CDA carefully

Make claims traceable to linguistic evidence, identify the social context being analyzed, and consider plausible alternative readings. CDA is an approach with varied methods; no single grammatical feature proves an author's intention or a text's social effect.
